## Uniform continuity and differentiation

### Key words

uniform continuity, Heine's theorem, differentiability, difference quotient, derivative, chord-slope function, differentiable implies continuous, sum rule, product rule, quotient rule, chain rule

### Reading assignment

Apostol, Chapter 4: the section on uniform continuity, and Chapter 5: the introductory sections on derivatives and the elementary differentiation rules.

## Practice problems

1. **The same formula on two different domains.** Consider

   $$
   f(x)=\frac1x.
   $$

   1. Let $$x,y\in[1,\infty)$$. Prove that

      $$
      \left\lvert\frac1x-\frac1y\right\rvert
      =\frac{\lvert x-y\rvert}{\lvert xy\rvert}
      \leq \lvert x-y\rvert.
      $$

   2. Use part 1 to prove directly that $$f$$ is uniformly continuous on $$[1,\infty)$$.

   3. For $$n\in\mathbb{N}$$, define

      $$
      x_n=\frac1{n+1}
      \qquad\text{and}\qquad
      y_n=\frac1{n+2}.
      $$

      Prove that

      $$
      \lvert x_n-y_n\rvert\longrightarrow0
      $$

      but

      $$
      \lvert f(x_n)-f(y_n)\rvert=1
      $$

      for every $$n$$.

   4. Use part 3 to prove that $$f$$ is not uniformly continuous on $$(0,1)$$.

   5. The function $$f$$ is continuous at every point of both domains. Explain why this does not contradict parts 2 and 4.

2. **Chord-slope functions.** Fix $$c\in\mathbb{R}$$ and let $$f(x)=x^2$$.

   1. Factor $$f(x)-f(c)$$ and prove that

      $$
      f(x)-f(c)=(x-c)(x+c).
      $$

   2. Define the chord-slope function

      $$
      f^*(x)=x+c.
      $$

      Explain why $$f^*$$ is continuous at $$c$$ and use the chord-function theorem to prove that $$f$$ is differentiable at $$c$$.

   3. Compute $$f'(c)$$ from $$f^*(c)$$.

   4. Now let $$c\ne0$$ and let $$g(x)=1/x$$ on an open interval containing $$c$$ but not containing $$0$$. Prove that, for $$x\ne c$$,

      $$
      \frac{g(x)-g(c)}{x-c}=-\frac1{cx}.
      $$

   5. Define

      $$
      g^*(x)=
      \begin{cases}
      -\dfrac1{cx},&x\ne c,\\
      -\dfrac1{c^2},&x=c.
      \end{cases}
      $$

      Prove that $$g^*$$ is continuous at $$c$$ and conclude that

      $$
      g'(c)=-\frac1{c^2}.
      $$

   6. Use the theorem that differentiability implies continuity to recover the continuity of $$x^2$$ and $$1/x$$ at every point of their respective domains.

3. **Continuity does not imply differentiability.** For $$\alpha>0$$, define

   $$
   f_\alpha(x)=\lvert x\rvert^\alpha.
   $$

   1. Prove directly that $$f_\alpha$$ is continuous at $$0$$ for every $$\alpha>0$$.

   2. For $$x\ne0$$, simplify the difference quotient

      $$
      \frac{f_\alpha(x)-f_\alpha(0)}{x-0}.
      $$

   3. Suppose $$\alpha>1$$. Prove that $$f_\alpha$$ is differentiable at $$0$$ and find $$f_\alpha'(0)$$.

   4. Prove that $$f_1(x)=\lvert x\rvert$$ is not differentiable at $$0$$ by comparing the difference quotient for positive and negative values of $$x$$.

   5. Suppose $$0<\alpha<1$$. Prove that $$f_\alpha$$ is not differentiable at $$0$$.

   6. Summarize what this family shows about the relationship between continuity and differentiability.

4. **Derivatives of polynomials and rational functions.** You may use the sum and product rules from the lecture.

   1. Use the product rule and induction to prove that

      $$
      \frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1}
      $$

      for every positive integer $$n$$.

   2. Use the product rule to prove the constant-multiple rule

      $$
      (cf)'=cf'
      $$

      for every constant $$c\in\mathbb{R}$$.

   3. Deduce that if

      $$
      p(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_nx^n,
      $$

      then

      $$
      p'(x)=a_1+2a_2x+\cdots+na_nx^{n-1}.
      $$

   4. Let

      $$
      R(x)=\frac{x^3-2x+1}{x^2+1}.
      $$

      Explain why $$R$$ is differentiable on all of $$\mathbb{R}$$.

   5. Use the quotient rule to compute $$R'(x)$$ and simplify the numerator.

   6. Compute $$R'(0)$$ directly from the definition of the derivative and verify that it agrees with your formula from part 5.

5. **Proving and using the chain rule.** Let $$f$$ be differentiable at $$c$$, let $$g$$ be differentiable at $$f(c)$$, and define $$h=g\circ f$$.

   1. Use the chord-function theorem to choose functions $$f^*$$ and $$g^*$$ satisfying

      $$
      f(x)-f(c)=(x-c)f^*(x)
      $$

      and

      $$
      g(u)-g(f(c))=(u-f(c))g^*(u),
      $$

      where $$f^*$$ is continuous at $$c$$ and $$g^*$$ is continuous at $$f(c)$$.

   2. Substitute $$u=f(x)$$ and prove that

      $$
      h(x)-h(c)
      =(x-c)f^*(x)g^*(f(x)).
      $$

   3. Explain why $$g^*\circ f$$ is continuous at $$c$$. Be sure to use the fact that differentiability of $$f$$ implies continuity of $$f$$.

   4. Apply the chord-function theorem to conclude that $$h$$ is differentiable at $$c$$ and that

      $$
      h'(c)=g'(f(c))f'(c).
      $$

   5. Use the chain rule and the power rule to compute the derivative of

      $$
      h(x)=(1+x^2)^5.
      $$

   6. Use the chain rule to compute the derivative of

      $$
      k(x)=\frac1{1+x^2}.
      $$

6. **Uniform continuity controls missing endpoints.** Let $$f:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}$$ be uniformly continuous.

   1. Prove that if $$(x_n)$$ is a Cauchy sequence in $$(0,1)$$, then $$(f(x_n))$$ is a Cauchy sequence in $$\mathbb{R}$$.

   2. Define

      $$
      a_n=\frac1{n+1}
      \qquad\text{and}\qquad
      b_n=1-\frac1{n+1}.
      $$

      Use completeness of $$\mathbb{R}$$ to prove that there exist $$L_0,L_1\in\mathbb{R}$$ such that

      $$
      f(a_n)\longrightarrow L_0
      \qquad\text{and}\qquad
      f(b_n)\longrightarrow L_1.
      $$

   3. Let $$(x_n)$$ be any sequence in $$(0,1)$$ satisfying $$x_n\to0$$. Use uniform continuity to prove that

      $$
      f(x_n)\longrightarrow L_0.
      $$

      Prove the analogous statement at $$1$$.

   4. Define $$F:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$$ by

      $$
      F(x)=
      \begin{cases}
      L_0,&x=0,\\
      f(x),&0<x<1,\\
      L_1,&x=1.
      \end{cases}
      $$

      Prove that $$F$$ is continuous on $$[0,1]$$.

   5. Use Heine's theorem to conclude that $$F$$ is uniformly continuous on $$[0,1]$$.

   6. Explain why the function $$f(x)=1/x$$ on $$(0,1)$$ cannot be uniformly continuous by comparing its behavior near $$0$$ with the extension property proved above.
